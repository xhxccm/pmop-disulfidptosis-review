# Section 5. Immune infiltration and single-cell data: recalibrating the strength of evidence

## 5.1 Deconvolution estimates are hypotheses, not measurements

Immune infiltration analysis has become almost mandatory in bioinformatics studies of osteoporosis. In the case analysis, a CIBERSORT-style nearest-normalized-fit estimation [11] based on the LM22 signature matrix suggested that naïve B cells were elevated in PMOP samples (log2fold change = 1.05, *P* = 0.005), whereas activated mast cells and monocytes were reduced. Correlation analysis then identified 39 significant relationships between Hub genes and immune-cell fractions (e.g., DHX35 with naïve B cells, *r* = 0.505; ADRB1 with activated dendritic cells, *r* = −0.473).

These results are often presented as evidence of an "immune mechanism". We argue they should be framed more cautiously. Deconvolution estimates **relative cell proportions** from bulk expression using a reference matrix derived from a different tissue and cell type. The LM22 signature was built from peripheral blood and cell lines; its applicability to bone tissue or even to the specific mononuclear-cell subset studied here is uncertain. Moreover, a correlation between a gene and an estimated fraction is a correlation between two derived quantities, each with its own error. Such analyses are valuable for **hypothesis generation** — they suggest which cell types merit attention — but they do not demonstrate that a Hub gene regulates a cell population, nor that the population causes the disease.

## 5.2 Single-cell data localize genes; they rarely validate disease effects

Single-cell RNA sequencing offers a more direct view of cellular context. The case analysis used GSE169396, a human femoral-head dataset. After quality control, 3,890 cells were retained and annotated into nine lineages; 17 of the 18 Hub genes were detectable. MAP3K1 was enriched in B cells and macrophages, OSTF1 in monocytes, macrophages, and T cells, and TM7SF3 in osteoblasts — providing a cellular interpretation of the bulk-level immune correlations.

However, two design limitations constrain the inference. First, only one sample was available, with no disease-group comparison. The analysis can therefore answer "in which cells is this gene expressed?" but not "is its expression altered in osteoporosis?". Second, the cell-type composition of a femoral-head sample differs substantially from that of peripheral blood; the single-cell data cannot directly validate a signature derived from blood. These are design constraints, not computational shortcomings — no algorithm can recover a disease contrast that was never sampled. We note that a recent study of the same disease combined bulk microarrays with single-cell data and reached a BHLHE41–SLC7A11 hypothesis [17], illustrating both the potential and the interpretive limits of this design.

## 5.3 Cell–cell communication from co-expression is a proxy, not a test

Because the original CellChat package was unavailable in the local R environment, cell–cell communication in the case analysis was approximated by ligand–receptor co-expression across 12 pairs (CXCL12–CXCR4, CSF1–CSF1R, TGFB1–TGFBR1, SPP1–CD44, among others). This approach identifies pairs of genes whose expression patterns are correlated across cell types; it does not infer the statistical probability of an actual interaction, nor its direction or strength. Formal tools such as CellChat [12] incorporate expression magnitude, receptor–ligand complex stoichiometry, and permutation testing. The co-expression approximation should therefore be reported explicitly as a **reduced-capability substitute**, and conclusions drawn from it should be limited to "potential communication axes worth testing".

## 5.4 A graded interpretation

Taken together, immune deconvolution, single-cell localization, and communication inference form a coherent **contextual layer**: they tell us where a signature might act and which cells might be involved. What they do not provide is causal evidence. Figure 6 formalizes this distinction by placing such approaches in Tiers 1–3 of an evidence hierarchy, below functional assays (Tier 4) and in vivo efficacy (Tier 5). The practical recommendation is straightforward: use these analyses to prioritize experiments, not to replace them.

# Section 6. Network pharmacology and molecular docking: the credibility boundary of correlation networks

## 6.1 Compound–target networks inherit the biases of their databases

Network pharmacology constructs a bipartite graph linking compounds to predicted targets, then identifies "core" nodes by topological centrality. In the case analysis, five compounds from Mongolian *Echinops* (quercetin, apigenin, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, palmitic acid) were connected to 99 targets, including 31 disulfidptosis-related genes and five Hub genes. Such networks are visually compelling and computationally inexpensive, but their reliability is bounded by database quality.

A bibliometric analysis of network pharmacology in traditional medicine identified exactly this problem: the recurrent dominance of quercetin as a "main active ingredient" across unrelated herbs can bias results and even mislead research directions, because databases do not faithfully represent actual composition, component–component interactions, or disease-state-dependent effects [24]. Independent work has further shown that target sets derived from different curated databases can disagree substantially, directly affecting downstream pathway conclusions [25]. These caveats apply with full force to the case analysis, in which quercetin was both the most connected compound and the best-scoring ligand — precisely the pattern that the literature warns can be an artifact of database composition rather than a pharmacological insight.

## 6.2 Docking scores rank poses; they do not establish efficacy

Molecular docking was performed for six receptors against five ligands using AutoDock Vina [14], yielding 30 blind-docking runs. The most favorable scores ranged from −8.27 kcal/mol (quercetin–FLT3) to −4.36 kcal/mol (apigenin–RAC1). Two limitations deserve emphasis.

First, no co-crystallized ligand was available to define the binding pocket, so the search box covered the entire protein surface. Blind docking is appropriate for exploratory ranking but cannot identify whether a compound binds a physiologically relevant site. Second, docking scores are approximate scoring-function outputs, not measured binding free energies. Differences of a few tenths of a kcal/mol are within the noise of the method. Accordingly, these values support **relative prioritization** — quercetin–FLT3 is worth testing before apigenin–RAC1 — but do not constitute evidence of target engagement, let alone therapeutic efficacy. A recent critique of the field reached the same conclusion, emphasizing that network results require experimental confirmation [24].

## 6.3 Topology is not mechanism

Selecting "core" compounds by degree or betweenness centrality assumes that highly connected nodes are functionally dominant. In pharmacological networks, high degree often reflects a compound's broad database annotation (quercetin is annotated against hundreds of targets) rather than selective potency. The case analysis therefore reports network results as a **prioritization layer**: they nominate which compound–target pairs should be tested first, and they are consistent with the enrichment results (focal adhesion, actin cytoskeleton). They are not, by themselves, a mechanism.

This distinction matters for review writing. A network diagram can illustrate a hypothesis; it cannot verify one. The correct narrative form is "the network nominates X as a candidate for testing", not "the network demonstrates that X mediates Y".
