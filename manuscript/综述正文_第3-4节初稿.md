# Section 3. From raw expression to a candidate list: a step-by-step dissection of the standard pipeline

> Figure 2 provides an overview of the seven-step pipeline and the pitfall attached to each step; the subsections below expand on each.

## 3.1 Differential expression in small cohorts: why moderated statistics matter

The overwhelming majority of public-database biomarker studies are constrained by an unfavorable dimensional ratio: thousands of features measured in tens of samples. In our worked example, the primary cohort (GSE56815) contained 40 postmenopausal individuals — 20 with osteoporosis and 20 controls — profiled across 13,038 genes after preprocessing. In this regime, ordinary *t* tests estimate per-gene variances from very few replicates, producing unstable standard errors and an inflated tail of spurious significance. Empirical Bayes moderation, as implemented in limma [8], addresses this by shrinking gene-wise variances toward a pooled prior, thereby borrowing information across genes and stabilizing inference when *n* is small.

Applying this approach to the case cohort yielded 333 genes at false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and 2,581 at nominal *P* < 0.05. A permutation-based check indicated that the observed signal was approximately four-fold greater than expected under label randomization, supporting the presence of genuine differential expression despite the modest sample size. This step illustrates a general principle: **the choice of statistical model is not cosmetic in small cohorts — it determines whether the downstream gene list is signal or noise.** Methods that ignore variance moderation (e.g., unmoderated *t* tests or naive fold-change ranking) should be treated with caution when applied to datasets of comparable scale.

## 3.2 Threshold choice is a biological decision, not a technical formality

A recurring and under-appreciated source of irreproducibility is the selection of significance thresholds. In the case analysis, intersecting the 333 FDR-significant genes with a curated disulfidptosis-related gene (DRG) set produced just **two** candidates — FLNC and PGRMC2. Relaxing the criterion to nominal *P* < 0.05 expanded the intersection to **nine** genes (ACSL4, ACTN1, AJAP1, FLNA, FLNC, PGRMC2, RAC1, SLC2A3, TM9SF2). These are not incremental differences; they represent qualitatively different biological stories. The nine-gene set, for instance, was strongly enriched for focal adhesion and actin-cytoskeleton terms, whereas the two-gene set offered a much narrower mechanistic narrative.

This sensitivity has two implications. First, thresholds should be justified in relation to the biological question and the cost of false negatives, rather than chosen by convention alone. Second, authors should report results under multiple thresholds as a sensitivity analysis — a practice that is still uncommon in this literature. We note that several published osteoporosis–disulfidptosis studies have built their entire narrative on a single threshold without such reporting [15,16], which limits the comparability of their candidate lists.

## 3.3 Functional enrichment: ORA and GSEA answer different questions

Over-representation analysis (ORA) and gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) are frequently used interchangeably, yet they rest on different logic. ORA tests whether a predefined gene list is enriched in an annotation category and therefore requires a threshold up front; GSEA evaluates whether genes in a set are concentrated at the top or bottom of a ranked list and is threshold-free. In the case analysis, ORA of the nine-gene intersection highlighted focal adhesion (30.5-fold enrichment, adjusted *P* = 3.1 × 10⁻⁴), adherens junction, tight junction, and actin-cytoskeleton organization — a pattern consistent with the actin-collapse mechanism of disulfidptosis [1]. GSEA on the full ranked list, by contrast, showed downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation (NES = −2.25) and mitochondrial autophagy, together with upregulated immune/inflammatory signatures.

The two views are complementary rather than redundant: ORA describes the biology of the selected candidates, whereas GSEA captures coordinated shifts that may be diluted below the ORA threshold. However, both are sensitive to gene-set version and size. In our re-analysis, an extended 53-gene DRG set yielded a non-significant enrichment (NES = −1.07), while the core set did not — a reminder that conclusions can hinge on curation decisions that are rarely reported in detail. Independent re-implementation with fgsea [10] reproduced the KEGG enrichment pattern almost point-for-point (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation NES −2.19 vs −2.25 in the Python prerank implementation), confirming that this particular result is robust to software choice.

## 3.4 Three feature-selection strategies and their underlying assumptions

Feature selection is where machine learning enters the typical biomarker pipeline. The three most common approaches embody distinct assumptions:

- **LASSO** (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) [5] applies an L1 penalty that drives coefficients to zero, effectively assuming that only a small subset of features carries signal. In the case analysis, L1 logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation achieved AUC = 0.9675, and stability selection across 100 resamples retained 38 genes at frequency ≥ 0.8.
- **SVM-RFE** [6] recursively removes the lowest-ranking features according to a linear support-vector classifier. It selected 67 genes in the case analysis but reported a cross-validated AUC of 1.0 — an outcome that, as discussed in Section 4, should be interpreted as a warning sign rather than a success.
- **Random forest** [7] ranks features by impurity reduction (Gini) and permutation importance, capturing non-linear relationships that linear methods miss. The case analysis used 1,000 trees and retained the top 60 genes by dual-criterion ranking.

The three strategies and their intersection are illustrated in Figure 4 using the case data. The intersection of the three algorithms yielded an 18-gene signature. Intersecting multiple methods is often presented as a robustness enhancement, but it is not a substitute for independent validation: different algorithms may converge on the same low-variance, technically convenient features, and agreement among selection methods says nothing about transportability across cohorts. **The 18-gene set should therefore be described as a cohort-internal feature combination, not as a validated biomarker panel.**

> **Box 1. Data leakage — the most common error that is rarely disclosed**
> Feature selection performed on the full dataset before cross-validation leaks information from the evaluation folds into the training process, inflating cross-validated performance. Correct practice requires *nested* cross-validation, in which selection is repeated inside each training fold. In our case analysis, selection was performed within the training cohort, and the reported internal AUC therefore reflects a genuinely held-out evaluation within that cohort; however, because the external cohorts were not involved in any tuning step, their poor performance cannot be attributed to this particular form of leakage. This distinction matters: it directs the explanation toward cohort heterogeneity (Section 4) rather than to a correctable coding error.

## 3.5 Where the pipeline becomes a template

The steps described above — download, moderate, intersect, enrich, select, intersect again, plot a ROC curve — have become a recognizable template in this literature. A PubMed search for disulfidptosis plus osteoporosis returned multiple studies following substantially the same design [15–19], several using the same GSE56815 cohort, machine-learning selection, immune infiltration, and molecular docking. The existence of this template is not itself a defect; it reflects a rational division of labor between computational screening and experimental confirmation. The defect emerges when the template's internal metrics are reported as if they constituted validation. Section 4 examines precisely this failure mode using the case data.

# Section 4. The central pitfall: overfitting, data leakage, and cross-tissue generalization failure

## 4.1 An apparently perfect model

The 18-gene signature was used to build a logistic-regression diagnostic model. Within the training cohort (GSE56815), five-fold cross-validation produced an AUC of **1.000** — perfect discrimination. Nomograms, calibration curves, and decision-curve analysis were all generated, and on internal metrics the model appeared exemplary. External evaluation told a different story: in GSE7429 (B lymphocytes, 10 cases / 10 controls) the AUC was **0.340**, and in GSE56814 (a different platform, 26 controls / 16 cases) it was **0.572** (Figure 5A).

The first external value is below the chance baseline of 0.5. This is not random noise around chance; it indicates that the association between the signature and the phenotype is **reversed** in that cellular context. Such a result is qualitatively different from the more familiar "AUC drops from 0.99 to 0.75" pattern, and it demands a mechanistic, not merely statistical, explanation.

It is worth emphasizing that this pattern is not unique to our re-analysis. Several published studies in the same disease area report high internal AUCs while either omitting external validation entirely or relying on a single independent dataset [15,16,18]. The case study is valuable precisely because it performs the external checks that are often skipped and reports the unfavorable outcome.

## 4.2 A systematic diagnostic workflow

When a model collapses externally, the instinctive response is to assume overfitting and increase regularization. We instead applied a three-step diagnostic sequence, which we recommend as a general protocol.

**Step 1 — Exclude simple overfitting via regularization scanning.** We retrained the model at three regularization strengths (C = 1, 0.1, 0.01). Training AUC remained 1.00 at every setting, while external AUCs stayed in a narrow band of 0.35–0.50 (GSE7429) and 0.50 (GSE56814). Because changing model complexity did not close the train–test gap, **overfitting alone could not account for the failure**. This step is important because it prevents misdirected remediation: tuning hyperparameters cannot fix a problem that is not a variance problem.

**Step 2 — Examine cohort provenance.** The training cohort consisted of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) on platform GPL96; GSE7429 profiled B lymphocytes on the same platform; GSE56814 used a different platform (GPL5175) on a related but non-identical cohort. Thus the two external evaluations varied *both* cell lineage and — in one case — measurement platform. Gene-expression programs are cell-type specific; a signature learned in a mixed mononuclear population need not transfer to a purified B-cell population, and the direction of individual gene effects may invert. This is the most plausible explanation for AUC < 0.5.

**Step 3 — Rule out implementation artifacts.** Because the original analysis was conducted in Python, we independently re-implemented the entire modeling workflow in R using glmnet and pROC. The three AUC values matched to four decimal places (1.0000 / 0.3400 / 0.5721), and an earlier limma re-implementation had likewise reproduced the Python differential-expression results exactly. Agreement between two independent codebases establishes that the observed failure is a property of the data and the biological question, not of a particular library's defaults.

## 4.3 Why an AUC near 1.0 should trigger scrutiny, not celebration

Perfect or near-perfect separation in a small biomedical cohort is statistically suspect. With tens of samples and thousands of features, a linear classifier can often find a hyperplane that separates the classes perfectly by exploiting idiosyncratic variation — batch effects, subtle differences in sample handling, or chance correlations — that has nothing to do with the disease. The phenomenon is well documented in the prediction-model literature, where an AUC of 1.0 is treated as a diagnostic warning requiring verification by permutation testing, external evaluation, and assessment of calibration [2,3].

Several mechanisms deserve explicit consideration: (i) **dimensional dominance**, where *p* ≫ *n* permits spurious separation; (ii) **batch confounding**, in which case and control samples were processed in different runs; (iii) **label leakage**, where a feature encodes the outcome indirectly; and (iv) **optimistic resampling**, where validation folds were not truly held out. In our case analysis, permutation testing confirmed that the expression signal was real but finite, and the nested structure of the validation excluded the third and fourth mechanisms — which is precisely why the external failure is informative rather than dismissible.

We therefore propose a reporting norm: **any internal AUC ≥ 0.99 in a cohort of fewer than roughly 100 samples should be accompanied by an explicit investigation of these mechanisms**, analogous to the way an implausibly small *P* value prompts checks for data-entry errors.

## 4.4 What AUC below 0.5 actually means

An AUC below 0.5 is often dismissed as "the model is worse than random" and discarded. The more interesting reading is that the model has learned a real association whose **sign is context-dependent**. In GSE7429, the signature score was negatively correlated with case status (correlation ≈ −0.30), meaning that genes associated with osteoporosis in PBMCs behaved oppositely in B cells. This is biologically plausible: immune cell lineages differ in expression of the very genes that constitute the signature (e.g., FLT3 and ADRB1 are not uniformly expressed across lymphoid and myeloid populations), and a score dominated by such genes will change meaning as the cellular composition of the sample changes.

Two practical consequences follow. First, a model that fails externally with AUC < 0.5 should not be "fixed" by simply flipping the prediction direction; doing so would produce an apparently acceptable AUC while resting on an accidental sign inversion. Second, the failure itself can be scientifically productive — it identifies **cell-type specificity as a biological finding** and motivates stratified analyses that a naive model would obscure. In the case analysis, subsequent single-cell inspection showed that several Hub genes were enriched in specific lineages, supporting this interpretation (see Section 5).

## 4.5 Regularization does not rescue a distribution shift

The regularization experiment deserves a broader methodological statement. Regularization — whether L1, L2, or elastic net — trades bias for variance within a fixed data-generating distribution. Cross-tissue generalization failure is a **distribution shift**: the relationship between features and outcome changes between training and evaluation populations. No amount of coefficient shrinkage can recover a relationship that has reversed sign. This is why the regularization scan produced essentially constant external AUCs while leaving the training AUC at 1.0.

The implication for study design is straightforward: if the intended use of a biomarker is across tissues, platforms, or populations, the validation strategy must sample that heterogeneity *by design*. A single convenient external dataset from the same center or cell type does not test transportability, and reporting it as "external validation" overstates the evidence. TRIPOD+AI [2] codifies this distinction through its separation of development and evaluation data and its emphasis on representativeness of the evaluation population. PROBAST and its AI extension [3] provide the complementary risk-of-bias framework for judging whether a reported validation is trustworthy.

## 4.6 Practical recommendations

Synthesizing the case analysis and the surrounding literature, we offer the following checklist for studies of this type:

1. **Pre-specify the external evaluation cohort** before feature selection, and report its tissue/cell type, platform, and population structure.
2. **Perform feature selection inside cross-validation** (nested CV) to prevent leakage; report this explicitly.
3. **Treat AUC ≥ 0.99 in small cohorts as a flag**, accompanied by permutation testing and an investigation of batch confounding.
4. **Report calibration and decision-curve analysis**, not discrimination alone; a well-calibrated moderate-AUC model is more informative than an uncalibrated high-AUC one.
5. **Report unfavorable external results honestly**, including AUC < 0.5, and interpret them mechanistically rather than suppressing them.
6. **Distinguish a cohort-internal feature combination from a transportable biomarker** in the abstract, discussion, and title — the case signature meets the first description but not the second.
7. **Follow TRIPOD+AI for reporting and PROBAST+AI for self-assessment** [2,3], depositing code and data in a persistent repository to enable independent verification.

The 18-gene signature examined here satisfies none of the criteria for a diagnostic marker beyond the training cohort. Its value, in the context of this review, is as a transparent demonstration of why the field's dominant template should be accompanied by these safeguards. **An honest failed validation contributes more methodological information than a successful internal validation that is never tested beyond its own cohort.**
