# Section 7. How far can computational and literature-based alternatives go?

## 7.1 A layered answer to a practical question

A question we are frequently asked by colleagues without access to experimental facilities is whether cell and animal experiments can be replaced by public databases, published literature, single-cell sequencing, or virtual knockout. The honest answer is layered rather than binary. Different experimental readouts sit at different positions on an evidence ladder, and the substitutions available to each differ accordingly (Figure 7).

**Readouts that can be substantially replaced.** Differential expression, prognostic association, protein localization, and co-expression can be addressed with public repositories (GEO, TCGA, GTEx, the Human Protein Atlas) and published datasets. These resources do not require new experiments and, when used with attention to tissue and platform compatibility, can provide robust correlational evidence. Similarly, the cellular localization of a candidate gene can often be resolved from public single-cell atlases; our case analysis used such data to show which lineages express the 18 Hub genes.

**Readouts that can be partially replaced.** The transcriptional consequences of perturbing a gene can be approximated using perturbation resources: DepMap provides CRISPR dependency across cancer cell lines, LINCS L1000 and CMap offer expression signatures following chemical or genetic perturbation, and Perturb-seq combines single-cell readout with pooled perturbation. These are powerful for **prioritization** — they can suggest which of 18 candidates is most likely to matter — but they are observational with respect to the reader's own system and cannot establish phenotype.

**Readouts that cannot be replaced.** Cell functional phenotypes — viability, reactive oxygen species, F-actin morphology, GSH/GSSG ratios, osteogenic differentiation, mineralization — require a real cellular system. The defining feature of disulfidptosis is an actin-cytoskeleton morphological change [1]; no transcriptomic dataset can report whether F-actin collapsed. At the organism level, in vivo efficacy, bone microarchitecture (e.g., BV/TV by Micro-CT), pharmacokinetics, and toxicity are irreducible to computation. A study claiming to "validate" a therapeutic mechanism without such data is making a claim its methods cannot support.

## 7.2 The minimum viable experimental design

The practical implication is not that computational work is dispensable, but that it should be used to **shrink the experimental search space**. In the case analysis, 18 Hub genes were reduced to a short list by combining three independent criteria: LASSO stability frequency, cross-tissue expression localization, and docking scores. A minimal causal test would then require only one or two candidates, assessed with a knockdown-plus-rescue design: silence the candidate, confirm that disulfidptosis markers worsen, and show that a candidate compound reverses the change. This single causal loop converts a correlational narrative into a mechanistic one at a fraction of the cost of testing all 18 genes or running a full animal study.

For investigators who cannot perform animal work, the honest option is to complete the cellular causal loop and explicitly acknowledge the absence of in vivo validation, rather than implying that the network or docking analysis constitutes validation. Figure 6 provides the vocabulary for stating this distinction clearly.

## 7.3 Reporting boundaries

Regardless of which substitutions are used, three disclosures should be standard. First, state the **source and tissue of every public dataset**, including platform and sample composition. Second, label any surrogate analysis explicitly — a co-expression network is not a CellChat result [12], and a blind docking score is not a binding constant [14]. Third, distinguish hypothesis-generating from confirmatory evidence in the abstract and discussion. These practices cost nothing and prevent the most common form of overclaiming in this literature.

# Section 8. Conclusions and recommendations for reporting

## 8.1 What the case study establishes

The worked example examined in this review produced a 333-gene differential expression signature, an 18-gene Hub set, and a diagnostic model with perfect internal discrimination but external AUCs of 0.34 and 0.57. A systematic diagnostic workflow showed that the failure was not simple overfitting (regularization tuning did not help), not a software artifact (two independent implementations agreed exactly), but most plausibly a **cross-tissue and cross-platform shift**: the model was trained on peripheral blood mononuclear cells and evaluated in B lymphocytes and on a different array platform. The 18-gene signature is therefore best described as a cohort-internal feature combination, not a transportable diagnostic marker.

We emphasize that this unfavorable outcome is not a defect of the analysis; it is the result of performing the validation that is often omitted. The methodological lesson generalizes: **internal performance metrics cannot establish transportability, and a candid failed validation is more informative than a successful one that is never tested beyond its own cohort.**

## 8.2 Recommendations

Synthesizing the case and the surrounding methodological literature, we offer five recommendations for studies of this type:

1. **Pre-specify evaluation data.** Identify an independent cohort before feature selection and report its tissue, cell composition, platform, and population structure. If none exists, say so explicitly rather than substituting the training cohort.
2. **Guard against leakage and optimism.** Perform feature selection inside cross-validation, use repeated resampling, and investigate any AUC ≥ 0.99 in a small cohort with permutation testing and batch-confounding checks.
3. **Report discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility together.** AUC alone is insufficient; calibration curves and decision-curve analysis are required to judge whether a model could influence decisions.
4. **Grade evidence honestly.** Use the hierarchy in Figure 6 to distinguish correlational, contextual, perturbational, functional, and in-vivo evidence, and match claims to the tier actually reached.
5. **Follow reporting standards.** TRIPOD+AI [2] provides a 27-item checklist for prediction-model studies; PROBAST and PROBAST+AI [3] assess risk of bias and applicability. Figure 8 condenses these into a practical checklist. Deposit data, code, and the exact computational environment in a persistent repository so that the analysis can be independently reproduced — as was done for the case study described here.

## 8.3 Outlook

The field's current template — download, select, model, plot — will remain attractive because it is fast and requires no wet laboratory. Its value will increase, not diminish, if the optional steps become mandatory: external evaluation, honest reporting of failure, calibration, and a clear boundary between prioritized hypotheses and validated markers. Single-cell atlases, perturbation resources, and open docking tools will continue to expand what can be learned in silico. But the interpretation of what those resources establish must remain disciplined. **A biomarker is not validated by a high AUC in the cohort that produced it; it is validated by surviving contact with an independent one.**
