# Section 1. Introduction: the rise and credibility crisis of biomarker discovery

## 1.1 A paradigm shift with an unfinished audit

Osteoporosis affects more than 200 million people worldwide, and approximately half of all postmenopausal women will sustain an osteoporosis-related fracture during their lifetime [23]. Despite this burden, the molecular basis of postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP) remains incompletely characterized, and reliable biomarkers for early identification of high-risk individuals are still lacking. Over the past decade, the combination of public expression repositories — principally the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) — with machine-learning classifiers has become the dominant strategy for addressing this gap. A typical study downloads a case–control expression matrix, applies differential expression analysis, intersects the result with a disease-relevant gene set, ranks features using one or more algorithms, and reports a diagnostic model with an impressive area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

The output of this paradigm is prolific. Searches combining "bioinformatics", "machine learning", and "biomarker" return tens of thousands of records, and nearly every disease now has a published gene signature. Yet the translation rate of these signatures into clinical practice remains low. This discrepancy — abundant candidates, scarce validated markers — is not primarily a problem of data volume or algorithm sophistication. It is a problem of **inference discipline**: the internal metrics that dominate these papers are frequently reported as if they established clinical utility, while the checks that would actually test transportability are omitted or treated as optional.

## 1.2 Three structural weaknesses

We argue that three interconnected weaknesses account for much of this credibility gap.

**First, dimensionality and sample size are mismatched.** Most public cohorts contain tens of samples but measure tens of thousands of features. In this regime (*p* ≫ *n*), a classifier can achieve near-perfect separation by exploiting idiosyncratic variation that has no relationship to the disease. An AUC of 0.99 in a 40-sample cohort should therefore be treated as a signal for investigation, not as evidence of diagnostic performance — a point we develop quantitatively in Section 4.

**Second, validation is often internal in name only.** Feature selection performed on the complete dataset before cross-validation leaks information from evaluation folds into model building, inflating cross-validated estimates. Even when this error is avoided, many studies report only a single internal validation and either omit external cohorts or select one retrospectively. TRIPOD+AI [2] now explicitly separates model development from evaluation and requires that evaluation data be distinct and representative; PROBAST and its AI extension [3] provide the complementary framework for judging risk of bias. These standards exist, but adoption in the bioinformatics literature remains uneven.

**Third, biological context is under-reported.** Gene-expression programs are cell-type specific. A signature derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells need not transfer to B lymphocytes, and a marker identified on one microarray platform may behave differently on another. Yet many papers describe cohorts only by GEO accession number, without stating tissue, cell composition, platform, or population structure. When external validation fails — as it frequently does — the explanation is often sought in model complexity rather than in the biological mismatch that actually caused it.

## 1.3 What this review contributes

Rather than surveying candidate genes, this review dissects the **workflow itself**. We use a complete, publicly reproducible case study — a disulfidptosis-related gene analysis in PMOP — to show where each methodological decision can change the conclusion, and how a rigorous audit can distinguish an overfitted model from a biologically non-transportable one. The case is not presented as a novel discovery; on the contrary, closely related studies have already been published [15–19]. Its value lies in transparency: every step, including the unsuccessful external validation, is documented and independently re-implemented in two programming languages.

Our specific contributions are threefold: (i) a step-by-step decomposition of the standard pipeline with the pitfall attached to each step (Sections 2–3); (ii) a systematic diagnostic protocol for interpreting external validation failure, illustrated with the case data (Section 4); and (iii) an evidence hierarchy that clarifies which experimental readouts can and cannot be replaced by public data, single-cell resources, or virtual perturbation (Sections 5–7). We conclude with a reporting checklist distilled from TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI [2,3] (Section 8).

# Section 2. Disease context and the choice of a case study

## 2.1 Postmenopausal osteoporosis as a methodological testbed

PMOP is a skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mineral density, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and increased fracture risk following estrogen withdrawal. Its clinical burden is substantial: with more than 200 million affected individuals globally and roughly half of postmenopausal women experiencing an osteoporosis-related fracture, the lifetime cost and morbidity are considerable [23]. Yet diagnosis relies largely on densitometric thresholds applied after significant bone loss has occurred, and treatment gaps persist even among high-risk populations [23]. These features make PMOP an appropriate testbed for biomarker research — and, because the available public cohorts are small and heterogeneous, an unusually demanding one.

The case analysis drew on four publicly available datasets: GSE56815 (peripheral blood mononuclear cells, 20 PMOP versus 20 controls;GPL96), GSE7429 (B lymphocytes, 10 versus 10;GPL96), GSE56814 (a related cohort on a different platform, GPL5175;26 controls versus 16 cases), and GSE169396 (single-cell RNA sequencing of human femoral head tissue). The first served as the discovery cohort, the next two as external evaluation sets, and the last as a cell-type localization resource. We note at the outset that these datasets differ in **cell lineage, platform, and cohort composition** — a feature that is usually treated as a nuisance but that becomes the central explanatory variable in Section 4.

## 2.2 Disulfidptosis: a recent mechanism with a compact gene set

Disulfidptosis was described in 2023 as a distinct form of regulated cell death [1]. In cells with high SLC7A11 expression, glucose starvation blocks the pentose phosphate pathway and thus NADPH regeneration, while cystine import continues. The resulting NADPH depletion prevents reduction of cystine to cysteine, causing accumulation of disulfide molecules and aberrant disulfide bonding among actin cytoskeletal proteins. The consequence is F-actin collapse, cell contraction, and death — a morphology distinguishable from apoptosis and ferroptosis, and rescueable by disulfide-reducing agents but not by reactive-oxygen-species scavengers [1]. Figure 1 summarizes this mechanism.

Two features make disulfidptosis attractive as a methodological case. First, the corresponding gene sets are compact — roughly 10 to 50 genes across published collections — so a complete analysis can be presented without the opaque feature spaces typical of genome-wide studies. Second, the pathway is mechanistically specific: its signature readout (actin-network collapse) is a **cytoskeletal phenotype**, not a generic stress response. This specificity is useful because it allows one to ask whether transcriptomic signatures actually capture the biology they claim to represent.

## 2.3 Why this case is representative, not exceptional

A PubMed search combining disulfidptosis with osteoporosis returns multiple studies built on a recognizable template: a GEO cohort, differential expression or weighted gene co-expression network analysis, machine-learning feature selection, a nomogram, immune infiltration, and molecular docking, sometimes followed by limited experimental confirmation [15–19]. Several use the same GSE56815 discovery cohort. Some report favorable internal metrics; few subject their signatures to genuinely independent evaluation. Related reviews have begun to map the immune-metabolic regulation of disulfidptosis in skeletal disease [20–22].

This concentration is scientifically useful: it means the case analyzed here is not an outlier but an instance of a widely used design. Consequently, the methodological lessons drawn from it generalize beyond this particular disease or gene set. We do not claim that the 18-gene signature described later is novel or clinically useful — a PGRMC2-centered study in the same disease has already combined bioinformatic screening with experimental validation and Mendelian randomization [18]. Our claim is narrower and, we believe, more defensible: **the standard template systematically overstates what its internal metrics establish, and a transparent worked example is the clearest way to demonstrate why.**
